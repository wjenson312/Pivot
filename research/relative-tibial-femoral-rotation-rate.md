# Relative Tibial–Femoral Rotation Rate as a Knee Joint-Loading Proxy

**Cycle 1 research brief — Pivot wearable leg sleeve (dual IMU: femur + tibia)**
Author: Researcher (PivotTeam) · Date: 2026-06-19

## What this cycle measures (plain-language summary for athletes/coaches)

Pivot has one motion sensor on your thigh (femur) and one on your shin (tibia). When your knee twists or bends, the two sensors rotate by different amounts. By subtracting the thigh sensor's rotation rate from the shin sensor's rotation rate, we get the **relative rotation rate across the knee** — how fast the joint itself is being twisted/flexed, separate from how fast your whole leg is swinging. We roll this into a **Knee Rotation Load Index** (the peak relative rotation rate in a movement, plus the cumulative amount over a session). A higher index means the knee is being rotated harder and more often — which the literature links to greater strain on the ACL and surrounding tissue. **This is a relative, qualitative load signal, not a calibrated joint torque in newton-metres.** It tells you *more vs. less* and *trending up vs. down*, not an absolute force value.

## BLOCKER — read before trusting any number from this method

**The whole metric is UNVALIDATED, and a firmware trace indicates the cycle-1 CSVs almost certainly hold ANGLES, not rates.** The CSV `imuN_g*` columns are NOT consistently the same physical quantity across firmware: `collectdata_dualIMU_BLE.ino` logs fused Euler **angles (degrees)** (getAngleX/Y/Z), while `bluetoothSync.ino` logs true gyro **rates (deg/s)** (getGyroX/Y/Z). This is method-breaking, not a footnote.

**Firmware trace (verified in-repo, corroborated by Critic):** the BLE sketch `collectdata_dualIMU_BLE.ino` transmits `getAngleX/Y/Z` (fused Euler **angles**), and the BLE receiver `logdata_dualIMU_BLE.py` blindly hardcodes the header `imu1_gx/gy/gz` (and `imu2_*`) onto those angle values — it documents the wire format as `g1x g1y g1z` (line 6) but maps the angle payload straight into those header columns (`vals = parts[1:13]`, header lines 28–32, mapping line 58). The repo's cycle-1 trial CSVs share this BLE header schema. Independent corroboration: in `Anterior_Rotation_RL.csv` the `g*` columns drift slowly and smoothly rather than oscillating like angular-rate signals — consistent with angles, not rates.

**Consequence:** treat the `g*` columns as **Euler angles (deg) unless the tester confirms a different collection path** (e.g. the `bluetoothSync.ino` SD path, which logs real gyro rates). Under the angle interpretation, `omega_tibia − omega_femur` is a relative **angle**, not a rate, so any "cumulative rotation-RATE dose" is dimensionally wrong; a rate must be derived by differentiating the relative angle (drift-/noise-prone, label lower-confidence). Differencing-then-direct-rate is valid only if the file is genuinely rate-typed.

**Action: Backend must auto-detect `channel_meaning ∈ {euler_deg, gyro_rate_dps}` per file (default to `euler_deg` for the BLE-header schema) and gate the index on it; the human tester must confirm which firmware produced each CSV and the gyro full-scale setting. Until then, treat all rate/dose outputs as provisional.** (Backend and Critic notified; Backend and Planner have the full firmware proof.)

## Subtopics, ranked by strength of supporting evidence

Scale: **well-established** (large, consistent peer-reviewed body) → **emerging** (growing but still maturing evidence) → **experimental** (plausible, limited or indirect evidence).

### 1. Tibial rotation and anterior translation load the ACL — **well-established**
The mechanical link between tibial motion relative to the femur and ACL strain is one of the best-documented findings in knee biomechanics. Cadaveric loading studies showed the ACL is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation and a secondary restraint to internal tibial rotation, with measured ACL force rising as these motions increase (Markolf et al., 1995, *Journal of Orthopaedic Research*; Markolf et al., 1990, *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery*). In-vivo strain-gauge measurements in conscious subjects confirmed that ACL strain increases with anterior tibial load and with combined rotational loading (Beynnon et al., 1995, *American Journal of Sports Medicine*; Fleming & Beynnon, 2004, *Annals of Biomedical Engineering*). For an athlete: when the shin rotates or slides relative to the thigh, the ACL is what stops it — so larger/faster relative tibial-femoral rotation means the ACL and capsule are working harder.

### 2. Combined rotational + valgus loading is a key ACL-injury mechanism — **mechanism: well-established / rotation-rate as a tracked injury-risk signal: emerging**
ACL injuries — especially non-contact ones — cluster around a multiplanar loading pattern that includes internal tibial rotation combined with knee abduction (valgus) near full extension during landing/cutting. Prospective cohort work identified high knee abduction loads as predictors of future ACL injury (Hewett et al., 2005, *American Journal of Sports Medicine*), and biomechanical reconstructions of injury events implicate a combination of compression, anterior shear, and internal rotation (Quatman, Quatman-Yates & Hewett, 2010, *Sports Medicine*; Koga et al., 2010, *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, video analysis of real injuries). **Important scope of the label:** the injury *mechanism* (rotation + valgus as ingredients of injurious loading) is well-established, but the prospective predictive evidence (Hewett 2005) is specifically for **knee abduction MOMENT (valgus)**, not for tibial-rotation rate. Treating relative tibial-femoral rotation rate as a *tracked* injury-relevant signal is therefore emerging, not well-established. For Pivot: the metric flags one recognised ingredient of injurious knee loading, but rotation alone is a *partial* picture (valgus and timing also matter), which is why we frame the index as a load *proxy*, never an injury predictor.

### 3. Cumulative/repetitive knee load drives overuse and tissue adaptation — **principle: well-established / this single-joint "rotational dose" operationalization: experimental**
Bone and soft tissue adapt to mechanical load following a dose-response relationship (mechanostat / Wolff's-law framework): moderate cyclic loading strengthens tissue, while load accumulating faster than the tissue can adapt drives overuse injury (Frost, 2003, *The Anatomical Record* — bone mechanostat; Schoenfeld, 2010, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* — connective-tissue loading). In sport-science practice, sharp spikes in cumulative training load relative to recent baseline are associated with elevated injury risk (Gabbett, 2016, *British Journal of Sports Medicine* — acute:chronic workload). **Scope of the label:** these are whole-body training-load and tissue-level adaptation principles — they are well-established. Operationalizing them by summing ONE joint's relative rotation over a short (4–7 s) clip and treating it as a "rotational dose" comparable to acute:chronic workload is an unvalidated extrapolation — that specific operationalization is **experimental**, not well-established. We use it as the basis for Pivot's **cumulative** Knee Rotation Load Index, but it must be presented as the athlete's own relative trend, not as a validated dose: the *threshold* between healthy and harmful is individual and not quantifiable from two IMUs, and the leap from established load-science to this per-joint metric has not been validated against tissue outcomes.

### 4. Relative segment angular velocity (gyro differencing) reflects joint angular velocity — **well-established (kinematics) / emerging (as a load proxy)**
That the difference between two rigid-segment angular velocities equals the joint's angular velocity is basic rigid-body kinematics and underlies standard IMU joint-angle estimation (Seel, Raisch & Schauer, 2014, *Sensors* — IMU joint axis/angle estimation validated against optical systems). So omega_rel = omega_tibia − omega_femur is a kinematically sound estimate of *knee* angular velocity, isolating joint motion from whole-limb swing — that part is well-established. Using that angular-velocity signal as a *loading* proxy is **emerging**: rotation rate correlates with the rate of tissue strain (faster joint rotation → higher strain rate, and ligament/tendon stiffness is strain-rate dependent — Pioletti & Rakotomanana, 2000, *European Journal of Mechanics*), but angular velocity is not force. It indexes how vigorously the joint is being driven, not the actual load path, which also depends on muscle co-contraction, ground reaction force, and joint angle. Hence: defensible as a *relative* index; would require force plates / inverse-dynamics modelling to become a calibrated load.

### 5. IMUs can validly measure knee angle and angular velocity in the field — **well-established (sagittal) / emerging (transverse rotation)**
Wearable IMUs estimate knee flexion–extension (sagittal) angle and angular velocity with good agreement to gold-standard optical motion capture during gait and dynamic tasks, typically within a few degrees RMS (Seel et al., 2014, *Sensors*; Cooper et al., 2009, *Journal of Biomechanics* — IMU knee angle vs. optical; Favre et al., 2008, *Journal of Biomechanics* — ambulatory 3D knee angle from inertial sensors). Two caveats matter for Pivot. First, **transverse-plane (internal/external rotation) IMU estimates are noisier and less validated** than sagittal angle, because small-amplitude rotation is more sensitive to sensor placement, soft-tissue artefact, and drift (Cutti et al., 2010, *Medical & Biological Engineering & Computing*). Second, **gyroscope integration drifts**, so working with the *rate* signal (as this cycle does) and with *relative* (differenced) quantities is the right call — differencing cancels shared drift/orientation error between the two sensors, and using rate avoids integration drift entirely. This supports the team's decision to use gyro-based relative rotation rate rather than accelerometer-integrated translation.

### 6. Why NOT accelerometer-derived translation this cycle — **well-established (methods limitation)**
Estimating linear tibial *translation* by double-integrating accelerometer signals is known to be unreliable: integration compounds bias and noise into rapidly growing position error, which is why inertial-only displacement tracking requires frequent zero-velocity updates or external reference (Woodman, 2007, *University of Cambridge Tech Report UCAM-CL-TR-696, "An introduction to inertial navigation"*; consistent with the drift behaviour described in Seel et al., 2014). This is the methodological reason — independent of the current dataset's frozen-accelerometer artefact — to scope Cycle 1 to rotation *rate* rather than translation. Anterior-translation metrics (clinically the most ACL-specific, per subtopic 1) are better pursued later with a calibrated approach, not raw accel integration.

## How to present the index to a user (evidence-consistent framing)
- Report it as **relative and trend-based** ("knee rotation load is 20% above your 7-day average"), never as an absolute force or an injury probability.
- Pair **peak** (single hardest twist — relevant to acute strain, subtopics 1–2) with **cumulative** (session dose — relevant to overuse/recovery, subtopic 3).
- State the sagittal-vs-transverse confidence difference (subtopic 5): flexion/extension load is more trustworthy than pure rotation load from these sensors.

## Open questions / caveats to flag to the team
1. **IMU mapping undocumented.** Which physical sensor (imu1 = mux ch0, imu2 = mux ch1) is on the femur vs. tibia is not recorded anywhere in the repo (firmware, code, README, docs). This must be resolved by the human tester / physical labelling before omega_rel has a defined sign convention. Empirically, the tibia (shank) shows the larger swing-phase angular excursion, which can disambiguate from data if needed.
2. **Column semantics differ by sketch — METHOD-BREAKING BLOCKER (see the BLOCKER section at the top).** In `collectdata_dualIMU_BLE.ino` the CSV `imuN_g*` columns are fused Euler *angles in degrees* (getAngleX/Y/Z), whereas `bluetoothSync.ino` logs true *gyro rates in deg/s* (getGyroX/Y/Z). The "relative rotation rate" method needs genuine rate data; if the file holds angles, the cumulative rotation-rate dose is dimensionally wrong. The index is unvalidated until per-file column meaning is confirmed/auto-detected. (Backend and Critic notified.)
3. **Accelerometer columns are frozen** in the Anterior/Posterior/Tibial rotation CSVs (constant repeated values), likely pre-dating commit 657b146 — reinforces the gyro-only scope this cycle.
4. **Proxy, not measurement.** Calibrated joint load (Nm) would require inverse dynamics with ground reaction force; that is out of scope for a two-IMU wearable and should not be claimed.
